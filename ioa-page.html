The Interactive Oral Assessment

A live, two-hour roundtable in which you discuss two MTC productions with your group and your tutors. The culminating assessment of the subject.

Date 27 May
Duration 2 hours
Location Founders Room, Southbank
Weighting 50% of final grade

The IOA is a conversation, not an exam. You and your group sit around a table with your tutors and work through two productions you have all seen — what they were doing, how well they did it, and why it matters.

What this is

A roundtable, not a presentation

The IOA brings your group of approximately eight students together with two tutors for a structured but genuinely open discussion of the two MTC productions you have seen this semester. It is the largest assessment in the subject and the one that draws everything else together — the modules, the productions, the tutorials, the Research Journal, the pre-IOA submission.

The format has three movements. You will each have up to five minutes to speak on your assigned focus area, drawing on the considered writing you submitted the week before. Your peers will listen, take notes, and may follow up with questions. Then the tutors will open the room to a group discussion in which everyone is expected to contribute meaningfully — extending arguments, testing claims, raising the questions you brought with you, responding to what others have said.

The mark you receive is for both your individual five minutes and your contribution to the discussion that follows. The two are weighted together and assessed against the same rubric.

The run of the day

How the two hours will unfold

The session moves through six phases. Timings are approximate and your tutors will adjust as the conversation requires.

10 min
Settling in
A short warm-up exercise to help the group get to know each other and settle into the room. Your tutors might ask you to share your favourite moment in the theatre, or your favourite ice cream flavour — something low-stakes to start the conversation moving.
45 min
Individual five minutes
Each student speaks for up to five minutes on their assigned focus area, comparing how it operated across both productions. Do not read from a prepared script. You may speak from dot points or brief notes, using them as prompts. Your tutor may ask you a follow-up question at the end.
15 min
Questions you have brought
Each student arrives with one or two questions they want to raise with the group — about one or both productions. You can ask your question during your five minutes, or save it for this part of the session.
15 min
Cross-cutting discussion
Your tutors will pick up the most generative threads from the individual presentations and open them up — moments where two students disagreed, observations that deserved more time, claims that warranted pressure-testing. Everyone is expected to engage.
15 min
Wider thematic conversation
Your tutors will introduce broader questions — casting, accents, funding, how the shows were received in the press, what they tell us about Australian theatre right now — and the group works through them together.
5 min
Drawing it together
A short close in which the group identifies the key positions, disagreements, and unresolved questions that have emerged from the conversation.
Your five minutes

Speaking on your focus area

Your tutor has allocated you one of eight focus areas. This is the area you wrote on in your Pre-IOA Submission, and it is what your individual five minutes will address. Speak comparatively — how did your focus area operate in each production, where did the two shows do comparable work, where did they part ways, and what is your considered judgement about each.

The eight focus areas
  1. Performances
  2. Impact
  3. Set design
  4. Costume design
  5. Lighting design
  6. Sound design and composition
  7. Direction and interpretation of the text
  8. Relevance, originality and theatricality
On reading from notes

Do not read a written script aloud. Speak from dot points or short prompts so the talking remains live. The room is small, the conversation is real, and a recited essay deadens both. If your written submission is strong, the talking will follow from it.

How you will be marked

The four criteria, and what each is looking for

Your performance across the whole two hours is assessed against four criteria. The full rubric with all band descriptors is available as a downloadable PDF below; what follows is a summary of what each criterion is looking for in the room.

30%
Identification and evaluation of stagecraft
Can you identify the dramatic elements of each production and make considered judgements about how they worked? This is the criterion that asks whether you were watching closely and whether you can say something specific about what you saw — design, performance, structure, atmosphere, the production's ambition and its execution.
20%
Knowledge of the creative team
Do you understand who does what in making a piece of theatre, and can you speak about the specific contributions of the people who made these productions? This is about recognising theatre as collaborative work and being able to talk about it with the right vocabulary and the right names.
10%
Research and accuracy
Have you done the work of finding things out — names, terms, context, prior productions, critical responses — and is what you say about them correct? This criterion rewards curiosity and the willingness to check.
40%
Engagement in the discussion
The largest single criterion, and the reason it is called Interactive. Can you make substantive, well-argued contributions to the group conversation — extending other people's points, disagreeing productively, asking sharp questions, building shared understanding? This is not about talking the most. It is about talking well, listening closely, and helping the conversation go somewhere.
If you are nervous

What to do, and what not to worry about

Most students arrive at the IOA with some level of nerves, including students who have done many of these before. That is not a problem. A few things worth knowing.

What if I forget what I was going to say?
Pause. Look at your notes. Take a sip of water. The room will wait. Recovering from a lost thread is part of speaking in public, and tutors are not marking smoothness — they are marking thinking. A genuine pause followed by a considered response is worth more than a rehearsed performance.
What if someone says what I was going to say?
Good. Build on it. Say where you agree, where you would push further, where you see it differently. The discussion is supposed to overlap and accumulate. Repeating an existing point adds nothing; responding to one adds a great deal.
What if I disagree with the tutor or another student?
Say so, and say why. Productive disagreement is one of the things this assessment is designed to surface. The rubric rewards engaged discussion, not consensus.
What if I haven't done as much background reading as others?
Be honest about what you have and have not engaged with, and bring genuine attention to the productions themselves. Specific, careful observation about what you actually saw is often more valuable than vague reference to things you half-read.
What are tutors not looking for?
Polish. Performance. The right answer. Vocabulary used for its own sake. Loudness. Confidence as a substitute for thought. None of these will help you. What will help is attention, specificity, honesty about what you noticed, and willingness to engage with what others say.

The IOA works when the room is curious. Come ready to talk about two productions you have thought carefully about, and to listen as closely as you speak.
